# 014. The catalogue was adopted on authority, and the first measurement retired half of it

Date: 2026-08-26
Status: Accepted. The claim is withdrawn; the rules stay.

## Context

Half of this tool is a compilation of one writer's voice. The other half was a
compilation of Wikipedia's *Signs of AI writing* — a catalogue of phrases said
to mark generated prose. It was adopted on day one, on authority, because it is
a good list assembled by people who had read a lot of the stuff.

Nothing checked it. The two `samples/machine-*.md` files that appeared to
confirm it were written by the author, to trip his own rules, which is a test
of how well he predicted his own tool and not a test of the catalogue.

Day four generated a corpus that nobody wrote to prove a point.

## Decision

**The claim that these rules detect machine writing is withdrawn.** It is
removed from the README, the page and the rule comments, and replaced with what
is now measured: *this tool checks a voice against its own rules; it does not
identify authorship.*

**The phrase rules stay.** The style guide has two parts and they still serve
the first. If the author writes `važno je napomenuti`, that is bad prose by his
own standard, and it remains bad prose whoever wrote it. What has changed is
the reason to run the rule: it was "this reads as generated" and it is now
"this is not how I write".

**Three rules separate the corpora and get their own section**, because a
finding this specific should be the most prominent thing in the repository
after what the tool is: `em-dash-density`, `bold-ratio` and
`sentence-uniformity`. `bullet-bold-shape` and `participial-close` also carry
signal and are noisier for reasons already recorded.

**The ceilings on the phrase rules are now uncalibratable, and the constants
say so.** `density.phrase-ceiling` no longer claims it should sit "where
machine-written text sits". It needs a corpus of prose the author rejects
whoever wrote it, which is a harder thing to assemble than either corpus this
project has.

## Why the catalogue was wrong, as far as this goes

It is not wrong about what bad prose looks like. Every phrase on it is a phrase
worth removing.

It is wrong as a description of a specific model on a specific date, and it
never claimed otherwise. The catalogue was assembled across several years and
several models, from text produced by systems that wrote very differently from
this one. `claude-opus-5` in August 2026 does not write `delve`, does not open
paragraphs with `Moreover`, and does not close essays with `In conclusion`. It
writes em dashes and bold lead-ins instead, which are the tells that survived.

This is a moving target and the tool cannot pin it. A catalogue of lexical
tells describes the register of the models that were current when it was
written, and register changes faster than the catalogue does.

## Consequences

The tool's honest description got smaller and more accurate. "Checks your prose
against your own style guide" is a claim that survives measurement.
"Detects AI writing" is not, and it was the more marketable of the two.

Roughly half the rule set is now known to carry no signal against this model.
That is not a reason to delete it — the rules serve the voice half — but any
future reader deciding whether to adopt this repository's approach for
detection should read this ADR first.

The tells that survived are typographic and structural, and that is a more
interesting finding than the one that failed. Em dashes, bold ratio and
sentence-length variance are hard to fake by search-and-replace and are not
tied to a vocabulary that shifts with each model. `sentence-uniformity` was
described on day one as "the hardest to fake"; it is now also among the few
with measured separation.

The costs:

**One model, one date, one prompting style.** The corpus is `claude-opus-5`
asked for a blog post by a bare subject line. A person prompting carefully and
editing the output would produce something else, and that is the realistic
case. Nothing here measures it.

**The finding cannot be maintained without re-measuring.** A newer model would
need a new corpus, and this repository has no lockfile pinning which model the
ceilings came from — the same gap ADR 003 identifies for lexicons, one level
up.

**Withdrawing the claim leaves a smaller tool with the same number of rules.**
Somebody reading the rule list will still see sixteen rules and reasonably
assume they all do the same kind of work. The README's table now marks which
ones separate; nothing in the code does.

## Alternatives rejected

**Delete the phrase rules.** They do the job the first half of the guide asks
for. Deleting them because they failed a job they were also asked to do would
throw away the part that works.

**Keep the claim and note the caveat.** A claim with a caveat is a claim. The
sentence "detects AI writing" would go on being quoted without the paragraph
under it.

**Tune the ceilings until the phrase rules separate.** There is nothing to tune
toward: five of them have a generated distribution of exactly zero. No
threshold separates a distribution from an empty one, and a number chosen to
look like it did would be the worst thing in this repository.

**Wait for a bigger corpus before withdrawing.** Fifteen documents and 18,612
words produced counts of zero, not counts that were small. A larger corpus
could move `leverage` from 2 to 20; it will not turn 0 into a distribution.
