# You Will Be Lonely in Month Three, and That Doesn't Mean You Made a Mistake

There's a particular kind of silence that settles into a new apartment around week seven. The boxes are gone. You've figured out which burner on the stove actually works. You know where the good grocery store is and where the bad one is that's closer, so you go there anyway. And then one Tuesday evening you're standing in your kitchen and it occurs to you that you have not spoken out loud to anyone since you ordered coffee that morning, and that the person who made your coffee did not know your name and will not remember your face.

Nobody puts that in the moving guides. The moving guides are about forwarding your mail.

## The vacation phase is real, and it lies to you

The first few weeks in a new city are genuinely wonderful, and they're also a kind of hallucination. Everything is novel, so everything is interesting. You walk different routes home just to see what's there. You try four restaurants in a week. You feel a low electric hum of possibility that makes you text your old friends things like *I think this was the right call*.

Enjoy it. But know that it's built on novelty, and novelty is a depreciating asset. When it runs out — and it will, somewhere between month two and month four — you're left with the actual thing: a city where you have no history, no default weekend plans, and no one who would notice if you disappeared for a week.

This is the part where a lot of people conclude they've made a terrible mistake. Usually they haven't. They've just arrived at the honest part.

## What you're actually mourning

Here's something that surprised me to learn: when people struggle after moving, they're often not homesick for the *place*. They're homesick for who they were in it.

In your old city, you had a role. You were the friend who always knew where to go for dinner, or the one people called for help moving furniture, or the reliable third for trivia night. You had a reputation, an accumulated set of inside jokes, a body of evidence proving you were a specific kind of person. Then you moved, and all of that evaporated, and now you're a stranger with no context — a person who has to *explain* themselves rather than simply be known.

That's a real loss and it deserves to be grieved as one. You didn't just change addresses. You got demoted from character to extra, and you have to work your way back up.

The good news is that this is also the whole point. It's the reason people move.

## Repetition is the actual technology of belonging

Everyone tells you to "put yourself out there," which is advice with all the specificity of "be healthy."

The thing that actually works is boring: go to the same places, at the same times, over and over, until you stop being a customer and start being a regular.

Belonging isn't built through big introductions. It's built through the fourth time the guy at the bagel place starts making your order when he sees you walk in. It's the running group where you've shown up eleven Saturdays in a row and someone finally asks what you do for work. It's the bartender who remembers you had a job interview. These are small things and they accumulate into something that feels, eventually, like a life.

Which means the single highest-leverage decision you can make in a new city is to pick a few places and *keep going back*, even when the first three visits feel pointless. They are supposed to feel pointless. You're playing a long game whether you like it or not.

## Say yes to things you would have declined

You have, in your old city, a well-calibrated system for declining invitations. It is efficient and it is correct, because you had enough friends and enough plans and your time was scarce.

That system is now actively harmful. Turn it off for a year.

Go to the coworker's girlfriend's art show. Go to the birthday party where you'll know one person. Accept the invitation from someone you're not sure you even like, because they might introduce you to someone you do. Almost every close friend I've watched people make in a new city traces back through two or three degrees to an event they almost skipped.

You can turn the filter back on later. You'll need it. Just not yet.

## Unpack completely

Some people keep a box or two sealed for a year. They don't hang art. They don't buy the nice shower curtain. There's a logic to it — *what if this doesn't work out* — but it's a self-fulfilling logic. A home you haven't committed to won't feel like one, and a city where you don't have a home won't either.

Hang the pictures. Buy a plant you'd feel bad about killing. Sign up for the library card. Learn the names of the neighborhoods and the local political scandal and which highway everyone complains about. Behave like someone who lives there, and you'll start to.

## The old friendships need a new operating system

Proximity was doing a lot of work you never noticed. Once it's gone, friendships that ran on "we bump into each other" quietly starve.

So switch them to something deliberate. A standing monthly call. A shared photo album. A group chat with a low bar for content. It feels artificial at first and then it just feels like how you talk to that person now.

You'll also learn something a little sad: some friendships were mostly logistical. They were built on shared circumstance and won't survive its removal. That's not a betrayal by anyone. It's just what those friendships were, and they were still good.

## The timeline, roughly

Everyone's is different, but the shape tends to be similar. Three months in, it's hard. Six months in, you have acquaintances and a routine. A year in, you have a couple of real friends and the city has stopped being a place you're evaluating. Two years in, you catch yourself giving a tourist directions without thinking, or feeling territorial about a restaurant, or saying "we" about a sports team you didn't care about before.

That's the moment. Not the arrival — the arrival is nothing, the arrival is just a truck and a key. The moment is much later and much quieter, and you almost always notice it in hindsight.

Give it the time it needs. Month three is not the verdict.
