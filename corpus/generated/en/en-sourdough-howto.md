# Sourdough Bread: A Guide for People Who Have Failed Before

Most sourdough tutorials skip the part where you fail. Mine didn't go well at first — my first three loaves came out of the oven looking like something you'd use to prop open a door. Dense, gummy, sour in the wrong way. The problem wasn't my technique. It was that nobody told me what a healthy starter actually looks like, so I was baking with a jar of sad flour paste and blaming my shaping.

So let's start there.

## Part One: The Starter (Where Most People Go Wrong)

A sourdough starter is a stable culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria living in a flour-and-water slurry. Making one takes about a week to two weeks. Making one that can actually leaven bread sometimes takes three.

**To begin:** In a clean jar, mix 50g whole wheat or rye flour with 50g room-temperature water. Whole grain flours have more of the microbial life you want, so they get things moving faster than white flour. Stir until it looks like thick pancake batter. Cover loosely — a jar lid resting on top, not screwed down.

**Each day after:** Discard all but about 50g of the mixture, then feed it 50g flour and 50g water. Yes, you're throwing most of it away. This is not wasteful so much as necessary; without discarding, you'd have a bathtub of starter by Friday and the acidity would climb high enough to inhibit the yeast you're trying to cultivate.

**The trap:** Around day two or three, your starter will probably erupt. It'll double, smell sharp and cheesy, maybe a bit like vomit. You will think you've succeeded. You have not. That early activity comes from *Leuconostoc* bacteria, which show up early, produce a lot of gas, and then die off as conditions acidify. Your starter will then go completely flat and quiet for several days, and this is the moment most people give up.

Don't. Keep feeding it. Somewhere between day five and day twelve, the real yeast population establishes itself and you'll see slower, more reliable rises with a smell that shifts from acrid to something like yogurt or beer or green apples.

**You're ready when** your starter reliably doubles in volume within 4–8 hours of feeding, and does this three days in a row. That predictability is the whole point. A starter that doubles unpredictably is a starter that will ruin your Saturday.

## Part Two: What You Actually Need

- A Dutch oven or heavy lidded pot (this matters more than almost anything else — it traps steam, which is what gives you a thin, glassy, blistered crust)
- A digital scale (volume measurements for bread are a lie)
- A bench scraper, or a wide putty knife, or determination
- Bread flour, which has more protein than all-purpose and will hold a better structure
- Optional but pleasant: a banneton, a lame, a thermometer

## Part Three: The Loaf

This makes one round loaf at roughly 73% hydration — wet enough to get an open crumb, dry enough that you can still handle it without weeping.

**Ingredients**
- 100g active starter (fed 4–8 hours prior, at its peak)
- 350g water, around 90°F
- 500g bread flour
- 10g fine sea salt

### Step 1: Mix (10 minutes, then 1 hour rest)

Dissolve the starter into the water, stirring until cloudy. Add the flour and salt. Mix with your hand — a claw shape works well — until no dry flour remains. It will be shaggy and unpleasant and stick to everything. That's correct.

Cover and rest one hour. During this time the flour fully hydrates and gluten begins forming on its own, which saves you a great deal of kneading.

### Step 2: Bulk Fermentation (4–6 hours)

This is where the bread actually happens: the yeast produces gas, the bacteria produce flavor, and the gluten network develops enough strength to hold both.

Every 45 minutes for the first three hours, do a set of **stretch and folds**. Wet your hand, reach under one side of the dough, stretch it up until it resists, and fold it over the top. Rotate the bowl a quarter turn and repeat, four times total. Then leave it alone.

You'll feel the dough change dramatically. The first set will be a sticky mess. By the fourth, it'll be smooth, elastic, and hold its shape when you pull it.

**How to know when bulk is done** — and this is the part that separates good loaves from bricks. Don't watch the clock. Watch the dough. You want it to have grown by roughly 50–75%, feel puffy and jiggly, and show a few bubbles on the surface and sides. If your kitchen is 78°F this might take four hours; at 68°F it could take eight. Temperature is the single biggest variable in sourdough, and nobody's kitchen is the same as the recipe writer's.

Under-fermented dough gives you a dense, tight crumb. Over-fermented dough spreads out flat and tears easily. Slightly under is more recoverable than over, if you have to guess.

### Step 3: Pre-shape and Rest (30 minutes)

Turn the dough onto a lightly floured counter. Using your bench scraper, fold the edges toward the center to form a rough ball, then flip it seam-side down and drag it in circles against the counter to build surface tension. Cover with a towel and rest 20–30 minutes. The gluten relaxes and makes final shaping much easier.

### Step 4: Shape (5 minutes)

Flip the dough over so the smooth side is down. Fold the bottom third up, fold the sides in over each other like an envelope, then roll it up from the bottom into a tight log or round. Pinch the seam closed. Flip seam-side down, drag to tighten, then transfer seam-side *up* into a floured banneton or a bowl lined with a floured tea towel.

### Step 5: Cold Retard (12–18 hours)

Cover and refrigerate overnight. This does three things: it develops significantly more flavor, it firms the dough so it's easy to score, and it means you bake in the morning instead of at 11 p.m. Skipping this step is possible but I've never had a loaf improved by it.

### Step 6: Bake (45 minutes)

Put your Dutch oven, lid on, into a cold oven and heat to 500°F (260°C). Let it sit at temperature for at least 20 minutes after it beeps — you want the cast iron genuinely saturated with heat.

Take the dough straight from the fridge. Turn it out onto a piece of parchment, seam-side down. Score the top with a sharp blade or razor: one confident slash about half an inch deep, or a cross, or whatever pattern you like. Confidence matters more than the design; a hesitant score drags and tears.

Lower it into the screaming-hot pot using the parchment as a sling. Lid on. Bake 20 minutes.

Remove the lid, drop the oven to 450°F (230°C), and bake another 20–25 minutes until the crust is genuinely dark — a deep reddish brown, darker than feels comfortable. Pale sourdough tastes like nothing. If you have a thermometer, the interior should read 205–210°F.

### Step 7: Wait

Cool on a wire rack for **at least two hours**. I know. But the interior is still setting as it cools, and cutting early gives you a gummy, pasty crumb that would have been perfect an hour later. Listen to the crust crackling as it cools instead. That's the sound of you having succeeded.

## When It Goes Wrong

**Dense, tight crumb with a gummy streak** — under-fermented. Extend bulk next time, or find a warmer spot.

**Flat, spread-out loaf that won't hold shape** — over-fermented, or your starter was past its peak when you mixed. Shorten bulk; use your starter when it's just cresting, not collapsing.

**Large tunnels near the top crust** — usually a shaping issue; you trapped a big air pocket. Degas a bit more during shaping.

**No oven spring** — check that your Dutch oven was fully preheated. This is the most common culprit by far.

**Pale crust** — bake longer and hotter than you think you should. Really.

## The Actual Secret

It's a variable I resisted for an embarrassingly long time: keep notes. Kitchen temperature, bulk duration, how the dough felt, how the loaf came out. Four or five entries in and you'll start seeing your own patterns — that your kitchen runs cold in February, that your starter peaks in five hours not four, that you consistently under-ferment.

Sourdough isn't hard. It's just a process with a lot of variables and a slow feedback loop. Once you've calibrated to your own kitchen, you'll be baking better bread than you can buy, and you'll do it without measuring anything but the flour.

Good luck. Feed your starter.
