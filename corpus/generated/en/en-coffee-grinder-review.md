# I Spent $160 on a Hand-Crank Coffee Grinder and I Have Feelings About It

I want to be upfront: three months ago, if you'd told me I would pay real money for a device whose entire job is to make coffee beans smaller, and that this device would have *no motor*, I would have laughed at you. I would have gestured at the $19 blade grinder on my counter — the one shaped like a small plastic silo, the one that sounded like a lawnmower eating gravel — and said, "It works fine."

It did not work fine. I just didn't know that yet.

## The Problem I Didn't Know I Had

Here's the thing about a blade grinder: it doesn't grind. It *chops*. It flails a little propeller around at high speed and produces a chaotic mixture of boulders and dust, and then you pour water over that mixture and hope.

What you get is coffee that tastes like two different drinks fighting. The dust over-extracts and goes bitter and ashy. The boulders barely extract at all and contribute a thin, sour note underneath. Your brain averages these into "coffee, fine, whatever," and you add milk, and you move on with your life.

I moved on with my life for about eight years.

The thing that broke me was a bag of Ethiopian beans a friend brought back from a roaster in Portland. The bag said "blueberry, jasmine, honey." I made a cup and it tasted like... coffee. Slightly sour coffee. I made another cup. Same. I started to suspect I was the problem, which is how these hobbies always begin.

## Why I Went Manual

I did the research spiral that everyone does. Two weeks of forum posts, a YouTube rabbit hole, a spreadsheet I am too embarrassed to describe.

The consensus entry point for electric is the Baratza Encore, around $170. Solid machine, universally recommended, boring in a good way. I almost bought it three separate times.

What stopped me was my kitchen and my roommate. My apartment is small, my counter space is a rumor, and I get up at 5:40 to a still-sleeping household. An electric burr grinder at 5:40 in the morning is a hostile act.

So I ordered a 1Zpresso JX-Pro. Hand grinder. 48mm stainless conical burrs. About $160 at the time. Small enough to hold in one hand, heavy enough to feel like a piece of machining equipment, which it essentially is.

## Unboxing It Felt Slightly Absurd

It came in a hard zip case, like something you'd store a nice camera lens in. Inside: the grinder, a little brush, a spare hex key. The body is knurled aluminum. The crank folds. The whole thing weighs about a pound and a half and has the cold, dense, over-engineered quality of a good flashlight.

I remember standing there holding it and thinking: *I have made a mistake. I have bought a fancy pepper mill.*

Then I ground 20 grams of the Ethiopian beans.

## The Part Where I Eat My Words

The first thing you notice is the sound. Not a scream — a low, granular crunch, and then a change in pitch as you get to the last few beans, and then nothing. It takes something like 35 to 45 seconds for a pour-over dose. It is genuinely pleasant. It's the sound of a thing working correctly.

The second thing you notice is the grounds. I dumped them into the filter and they were *uniform*. Not one boulder. Barely any dust. Just a pile of consistent, fluffy particles that looked like they'd been sorted by a very small, very patient bureaucrat.

The coffee was a different drink. I'm not going to claim I tasted blueberry — I still don't fully trust my palate — but the sourness was gone, replaced by something bright and clean, with an actual floral thing happening at the back end. It was sweet in a way I had not previously known coffee could be sweet without adding anything to it.

I made a second cup immediately, which was a caffeine decision I regretted around 2 p.m.

## Living With It, Three Months In

**The grind adjustment is genuinely great.** There's an external dial with clicks — 12.5 microns each, 40 clicks per rotation — and it's numbered, so you can write down "3 rotations + 6 clicks" for your pour-over and "1 rotation + 2" for espresso and actually return to it. No guessing, no removing the burr to fiddle.

**The "arm workout" thing is overblown.** Everyone warns you about this. It's forty seconds of light cranking. Medium roasts are effortless. Very dark, oily beans take a bit more shoulder. If you have a wrist injury or arthritis, this is a real consideration and you should buy the electric — I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But for most people it's less effort than opening a stiff jar.

**The ritual is the surprise.** This is the part I didn't expect and can't fully defend. Standing at the counter for forty seconds, cranking, in the dark, before anything else has happened in the day — it turns out I like that. It's the one part of my morning that can't be optimized or done while looking at my phone. It requires two hands.

**The annoyances, honestly:** It's slow if you're making coffee for four people. Twenty grams is fine; eighty grams is a chore, and by the third dose you're doing math about your life choices. Static can make grounds cling to the catch cup — a single drop of water on the beans before grinding (the "RDT" trick) fixes this completely. And the folding crank has a small amount of play in it, which is normal and which bothered me for exactly one week.

## Was It Worth $160?

Here's the calculus that convinced me, and I think it's the right one: I was already spending $18 to $22 on a bag of good beans every couple of weeks, and destroying roughly half of what I paid for at the grinding stage. The grinder isn't really a $160 purchase. It's the thing that makes every subsequent bag of coffee worth what it costs.

The other honest answer is that I've made maybe 250 cups of coffee on it so far. That's about 64 cents a cup in amortized grinder, dropping every morning. My local place charges $5.25 for a pour-over that, and I say this with genuine humility about my own skill level, is not obviously better than what I make at home now.

## If You're Considering One

Buy the grinder before you buy anything else. Before the gooseneck kettle, before the scale, before the fancy dripper. Grind quality is the single biggest jump in cup quality available to a home brewer, and it isn't close. If you're currently on a blade grinder, upgrading is not an incremental improvement — it's a different beverage.

Go electric if you make coffee for multiple people, have any hand or wrist issues, or know yourself well enough to know that a forty-second manual task will eventually feel like a chore. The Encore is the safe answer and it's a good one.

Go manual if you're brewing one or two cups, you're tight on counter space, you value quiet mornings, or you suspect — as I apparently did, without knowing it — that you'd enjoy having one small physical ritual in your day.

I'll report back in a year, assuming I haven't spiraled into buying a refractometer.
