# I Ran My First Marathon and It Was Nothing Like I Expected

I signed up for a marathon the way most people do something stupid: at 11 p.m., alone, slightly emotional, with a credit card within reach.

A friend had just finished one. Her race photos were all sweat and joy and a foil blanket, and I remember thinking, *I want to know what that feels like.* Eighteen weeks later, I found out. It felt like being hit by a very slow truck. It was also one of the best days of my life.

Here's what I wish someone had told me.

## The training is the actual marathon

Everyone talks about race day. Nobody talks about the Tuesday in August when it's 84 degrees at 6 a.m., you have eight miles on the plan, and your legs feel like they were installed backwards.

That's the real thing. Race day is the party. Training is paying for the party.

I ran four days a week for four months. Most of those runs were unremarkable. Some were genuinely bad. A few — maybe six or seven total — were transcendent, the kind where you finish and feel like you've been let in on a secret. The ratio is roughly 1 great run to every 10 fine ones and 3 miserable ones, and you sign up for all of them equally.

The mental shift that helped me most: I stopped treating each run as a test I could pass or fail and started treating them as deposits. Bad run, still a deposit. Slow run, still a deposit. The bank doesn't care about your mood.

## Things nobody tells you

**Your long run pace should feel embarrassingly slow.** I spent my first six weeks running everything at roughly the same medium-hard effort, which is a great way to be permanently tired and slightly injured. Once I slowed my long runs down by nearly two minutes per mile, everything got better. It felt like cheating. It was not cheating.

**Chafing is a real, load-bearing concern.** I learned this at mile 14 of an 18-miler, in a way I will not describe further. Buy the anti-chafe balm. Apply it to places you don't think you need to. Then apply more.

**You have to practice eating.** Your stomach needs training just like your legs do. I found this out by taking three gels in quick succession during a long run and spending the remainder of the morning making urgent decisions about public restrooms. Figure out your fueling in training, not on race day.

**The taper will make you feel insane.** The final two to three weeks, you cut your mileage way back so your body can absorb all that work. Everyone told me I'd feel great. Instead I felt sluggish, anxious, and convinced I'd lost all my fitness overnight. My knee started hurting for no reason. I developed a phantom cough. This is apparently universal. The taper crazies are a rite of passage.

## Race morning

I woke up at 4:40 a.m. before my alarm, ate a bagel with peanut butter that I did not want, and stood in my kitchen in the dark feeling like I was about to take an exam I hadn't studied for.

The start line was cold, crowded, and weirdly joyful — 8,000 people in trash bags and throwaway sweatshirts, all doing nervous little hops. The guy next to me was running his fourteenth marathon. The woman on my other side was running her first, like me, and we made the kind of instant, intense friendship that exists only in porta-potty lines.

Then the gun went off and I did the single most common rookie mistake: I ran too fast.

## Miles 1–13: the lie

The first half of a marathon is a beautiful lie. You feel amazing. The crowd is loud, the adrenaline is real, and you've been resting for two weeks. My plan said 10:30 per mile. I ran the first mile at 9:15 and thought, *huh, I guess I'm just better than I thought.*

I was not better than I thought. I was excited.

I got myself mostly under control by mile 4, but those early fast miles are a loan with brutal interest, and I paid it back later with everything I had. If you take one thing from this post: **run the first half slower than feels right.** Then slower than that.

## Miles 14–19: the work

This is where the marathon actually begins. The crowds thin out. The adrenaline is gone. You settle into a rhythm and the whole thing becomes an honest conversation between you and your legs.

I liked this part. It was quiet and hard and clear. I ate my gels on schedule, took water at every station, and thought about almost nothing. Somewhere around mile 17 a man in a bathrobe was handing out orange slices and yelling "YOU'RE ALL BEAUTIFUL," and I got unexpectedly choked up.

## Miles 20–23: the wall

I'd read about the wall. I understood it intellectually — glycogen depletion, the body switching fuel sources, blah blah. I did not understand it *in my body* until mile 21, when my legs simply stopped being interested.

It wasn't pain, exactly. It was a total, negotiation-proof heaviness. Every step required a conscious decision. My pace fell apart. My thoughts got small and strange — I remember spending an entire mile thinking about a specific bowl of soup.

Here's what got me through: I stopped thinking about the finish line, which was an unbearable distance away, and started thinking about the next lamppost. Then the next one. Then the aid station. The marathon became roughly 400 tiny races, and I only had to win the one I was in.

Also, honestly: I walked through two aid stations. I'd told myself I wouldn't. It turns out nobody cares, least of all you, and thirty seconds of walking bought me two more miles of running.

## Mile 24 to the finish

Something switches on near the end. I don't know if it's the crowds thickening or the smell of the finish or just the arithmetic finally working in your favor — *only two miles, I run two miles for fun* — but I found a gear I didn't know I still had.

The last half mile was lined four deep with strangers screaming my name off my bib. I passed a woman holding a sign that said "YOU TRAINED FOR THIS" and I nearly came apart.

I crossed in 4:32:18. Thirty-two minutes slower than my goal.

I could not have cared less.

## What I'd tell someone signing up tonight at 11 p.m.

- **Pick a plan and mostly follow it.** Consistency beats optimization. A mediocre plan you complete crushes a perfect plan you abandon.
- **Slow down.** Both in training and in the first ten miles of the race.
- **Practice everything.** Shoes, socks, shorts, breakfast, gels, playlist. Nothing new on race day, including the enthusiasm.
- **Your goal time is a hypothesis, not a promise.** Weather, sleep, and luck all get a vote. Have a "great day" goal, a "normal day" goal, and a "just finish" goal.
- **Tell people.** The accountability is real, and you'll want them at mile 24.
- **Take the photo at the finish.** Yes, you look destroyed. That's the point.

Six months later, I still think about mile 21. Not fondly, exactly — but the way you think about any hard thing you found out you could do. I'd spent years quietly assuming that marathon runners were a different species. Turns out they're mostly just people who ran on a lot of Tuesdays.

I'm signed up for another one in the spring.
