# How to Set Up a Monthly Budget (That You'll Actually Stick To)

Most budgets fail in week three. Not because people lack discipline, but because the budget was fiction from the start — a wish list built on what someone *hoped* they spent rather than what they actually spend.

A good budget isn't a diet. It's a plan for money you've already earned. Here's how to build one that survives contact with real life.

## Step 1: Find your real take-home pay

Start with what actually lands in your bank account, not your salary. Look at your last few pay stubs and note the net amount after taxes, health insurance, and retirement contributions.

If you're paid biweekly, resist the urge to multiply by two. You get 26 paychecks a year, not 24. Multiply one paycheck by 26 and divide by 12 to get your true monthly average — then treat those two "extra" paycheck months as bonuses for savings or debt, not as normal income.

If your income varies, use the average of your lowest three months from the past year. Budget on that number. Anything above it is surplus, and surplus is a nice problem to have.

## Step 2: Audit what you actually spend

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that makes everything else work.

Pull up the last two to three months of bank and credit card statements. Go through every transaction and sort it into categories: rent, groceries, restaurants, gas, subscriptions, whatever fits your life. A spreadsheet works fine. So does a legal pad.

You're looking for two things: your baseline spending in each category, and the surprises. Almost everyone finds something — a $14 streaming service they forgot about, or the realization that "occasional" takeout is running $340 a month.

Don't judge yourself here. You're collecting data, not filing a confession.

## Step 3: Sort everything into three buckets

**Fixed costs** stay roughly the same each month: rent or mortgage, insurance, phone, internet, loan minimums, subscriptions. These are predictable, which makes them easy to plan around — and worth attacking when you need to free up money, because cutting one fixed cost saves you every single month.

**Variable costs** fluctuate: groceries, gas, dining out, entertainment, clothes. This is where budgets actually get managed, because it's where you have real decisions to make.

**Savings and debt payoff** is everything going toward your future: emergency fund, retirement, extra debt payments, sinking funds for known-but-irregular expenses.

## Step 4: Pick a framework

You don't need to invent a system from scratch. Three common ones:

**50/30/20** — 50% to needs, 30% to wants, 20% to savings and debt. Simple, good for beginners. The catch: if you live somewhere with high housing costs, hitting 50% on needs may be mathematically impossible. Use it as a direction, not a rule.

**Zero-based budgeting** — every dollar gets a job until income minus allocations equals zero. More work, more control. Best if you've tried loose budgeting and it didn't stick.

**Pay yourself first** — automate savings the day you get paid, then spend the rest freely. Least restrictive, and honestly effective for people who hate tracking. The tradeoff is less visibility into where the rest goes.

Most people end up somewhere in between: automated savings plus a handful of watched categories.

Two things worth noticing.

First, the **sinking funds**. Car repairs, holiday gifts, the annual dentist visit, a friend's wedding — these aren't emergencies, they're predictable expenses that just don't happen monthly. Setting aside $75 a month means the $900 brake job is annoying instead of catastrophic. This single habit prevents more budget collapses than any other.

Second, the **buffer**. That $120 with no assigned purpose is deliberate. Every budget gets hit by something unplanned. Building in slack means a bad week doesn't blow up the whole system.

## Step 6: Choose how you'll track it

Whatever you pick, it has to be low-friction enough that you'll still be doing it in March.

- **Apps** (YNAB, Monarch, Copilot, or your bank's built-in tools) sync automatically and categorize for you. Easiest to maintain, though the good ones cost money.
- **Spreadsheets** are free and infinitely customizable. Better if you like seeing the mechanics.
- **Cash envelopes** are old-fashioned but unbeatable for problem categories. Hard to overspend $200 on dining out when you've physically got $200.
- **Separate accounts** work well without any tracking at all: one checking account for fixed bills, one for spending money, one savings account. Automate transfers on payday and spend from the spending account guilt-free.

## Step 7: Do a monthly check-in

Put 30 minutes on your calendar at the end of each month. Compare what you planned to what happened, and ask one question about each variance: *is this number wrong, or was this month unusual?*

If you budgeted $450 for groceries and spent $600 three months running, the budget is wrong. Fix it — and pull the difference from somewhere else. A budget you keep violating isn't holding you accountable, it's just making you feel bad.

Expect your first two or three months to be rough drafts. That's normal. Month four is usually when it starts feeling automatic.

## Common mistakes to avoid

**Budgeting your aspirations.** If you spend $300 a month on restaurants, don't write down $75. Write $250 and work downward.

**Forgetting annual expenses.** Car registration, insurance premiums paid in full, Amazon Prime, property taxes. Add them up, divide by 12, and put them in sinking funds.

**No fun money.** A budget with zero discretionary spending is a budget you'll abandon. Even $50 a month of genuinely no-questions-asked money makes the whole thing sustainable.

**Skipping the emergency fund.** Start with $1,000, then work toward three to six months of essential expenses. Without it, every setback becomes new credit card debt, and you're back where you started.

**Quitting after a bad month.** One blown month doesn't invalidate the system. Reset and keep going.

## Start this week

You don't need perfect information to begin. Pull last month's statements, add up your fixed costs, pick a rough number for groceries and gas, and automate one savings transfer — even if it's $25.

The point of a budget isn't restriction. It's knowing that the money going out is going where you actually want it to go. That clarity tends to be worth more than the dollars it saves you.
