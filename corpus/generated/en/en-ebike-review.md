# One Year, 2,740 Miles: What I Actually Learned From My E-Bike

*A long-term review nobody asked for*

Last March I spent $2,300 on an electric bike — a mid-drive commuter with a 500Wh battery, eight gears, hydraulic discs, and a rack rated for 55 pounds. I told my wife it would pay for itself. She made a face that I have since learned to interpret as "I'm going to let you find out."

It's been twelve months. Here's the honest accounting.

## The numbers

- **Odometer:** 2,740 miles
- **Estimated car trips replaced:** ~410
- **Gas saved:** roughly 110 gallons, call it $380
- **Money spent on the bike since purchase:** $612 (breakdown below)
- **Times I've been genuinely soaked:** 6
- **Times I've said "I love this thing" out loud, alone, on a hill:** uncountable

So no, it hasn't paid for itself in dollars. It has paid for itself in a currency I didn't know I was short on, which I'll get to.

## The thing nobody tells you about the first two weeks

The first two weeks are a honeymoon and they are also a lie.

For fourteen days I rode everywhere. I invented errands. I went to a hardware store four miles away to buy a single washer. Hills that used to be *decisions* became scenery. I averaged 18 mph on a route where I used to average 11, and I arrived without needing a shirt change, which turns out to be the entire ballgame for commuting.

Then the novelty burned off and the real relationship started — the one where you notice the bike weighs 55 pounds and your apartment building's bike room is down five stairs. Where you learn that "60 mile range" means 60 miles at the lowest assist level on flat ground with a tailwind and a 130-pound rider, and that your actual range on assist level 3, with groceries, in November, is 34 miles.

That's not a scandal. It's just physics. But I wish the marketing had told me the honest number, because 34 miles is *still plenty* and I'd have trusted the brand more.

## What actually changed

Here's the thing I didn't predict: the e-bike didn't replace my bike rides. It replaced my *car* rides. Specifically, the two- to five-mile trips that are too far to walk with a gallon of milk and too short to feel like anything but a waste of a car.

The grocery store. The pediatrician. Returning library books. Dropping off dry cleaning. My daughter's swim lessons, which involve a trailer, a wet towel, and a child who falls asleep on the ride home with a goldfish cracker fused to her cheek.

Our second car sat in the driveway so long last summer that the battery died. We sold it in September. *That's* where the money is — not gas, but the $340/month of insurance, registration, maintenance, and depreciation we stopped paying. The e-bike paid for itself in about seven months once you count the car we didn't need.

I also just... feel better. I get twenty-five minutes of moving air on my face most days. I know which houses on Linden Street have the good gardens. I have a nemesis: a man on a gravel bike who passes me on the flats and whom I destroy on the bridge climb. He knows. I know he knows.

## Maintenance: the part reviews skip

Here's the actual spend:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Two sets of brake pads | $58 |
| One rear tire (glass) + two tubes | $94 |
| Chain (replaced at 1,800 mi) | $32 |
| Cassette (should have replaced chain sooner) | $71 |
| Two shop tune-ups | $190 |
| Better lights, fenders, a real lock | $167 |
| **Total** | **$612** |

Two lessons in there. First: an e-bike is a normal bike that goes faster and weighs more, which means it eats consumables about twice as fast. Brake pads especially. Budget for it. Second: replace your chain on schedule. I didn't, the chain stretched, and it chewed the cassette. That's a $71 lesson in "the drivetrain is a system."

The electrical side has been boringly reliable, which is what you want. One firmware update. One loose display connector I fixed by unplugging and replugging it while swearing. The motor has never made a noise I didn't like.

## Battery, after a year

This is what people ask most. My battery reports 94% of original capacity after roughly 190 full-equivalent cycles. Practically, that's a mile or two of lost range. I can't feel it.

What I think helped: I stopped charging to 100% unless I needed the range, I never left it dead over a weekend, and I brought it inside on nights below freezing instead of leaving it in the garage. Whether that's good practice or superstition, I don't know for sure — but it's cheap superstition.

## The genuine annoyances

**Weight.** Fifty-five pounds is fine rolling and miserable lifting. Stairs are a negotiation. If you live in a third-floor walk-up, take this seriously — it's the number one reason I hear about e-bikes gathering dust.

**Theft anxiety.** It's the most expensive thing I own that lives outdoors. I bought a heavy chain lock, I never leave it overnight anywhere but home, and I still take a photo of the serial number every time I get paranoid. Two people in my neighborhood group have lost theirs. This is a real cost of ownership, and it's mostly psychological.

**Cold.** Below about 25°F the range drops maybe 30%, the display gets sluggish, and my hands hurt regardless of gloves. Winter riding is absolutely doable — I did it — but it's a different sport, and I rode maybe half as much in January.

**Speed differential.** At 20 mph you're moving at car pace in a lane designed for 12 mph bikes and 3 mph pedestrians. You have to actively ride *slower* than the bike wants on mixed-use paths. Not everyone does, and the people who don't are why some paths now have signs about us.

## What I'd do differently

- **Buy from a shop with a service department.** Mine has fixed things under warranty in three days that would have been a six-week email chain with a direct-to-consumer brand.
- **Get fenders and real lights on day one.** Don't ride for four months planning to add them "eventually." Eventually is a wet sock.
- **Buy the rack and bags immediately.** A backpack works for a week. Panniers change what the bike is *for*.
- **Skip the throttle debate.** I thought I'd want one. I've used mine maybe twice, both times starting from a dead stop on a steep hill with cargo. Pedal assist is the whole product.

## Would I buy it again

Yes, and I'd buy it sooner.

Not because it's a hobby — I'm not really a cyclist, I don't own lycra, my heart rate does not go on the internet. But because for a year now, when I've needed to go two miles, the answer has just been *go*. No keys, no parking, no circling the block. The bike removed a small amount of friction from about four hundred moments, and it turns out that adds up to something that feels a lot like freedom.

The honeymoon ended in week three. I still like it in year two. That's about the best review I know how to give.
