# Before You Sign: A Renter's Checklist for Reading the Fine Print

There's a specific kind of pressure that hits when you finally find an apartment you like. The agent is standing there with a pen. Two other people looked at it this morning. The lease is twelve pages of dense text and you have, apparently, about four minutes to review it.

Sign anyway and you might be fine. Or you might discover in month three that you're on the hook for the water heater, that your "included" parking spot is $85/month, or that the lease renews automatically for another year unless you gave notice sixty days ago.

Here's what to actually check — the property, the paperwork, and the people — before your signature makes it official.

## Start With the Unit Itself

**Visit in person, and visit twice if you can.** Once during the day, once in the evening. The quiet building at 2 p.m. on a Tuesday can be a different place at 9 p.m. when everyone's home, the upstairs neighbor's kid is practicing drums, and the bar downstairs opens its patio.

While you're there, be the annoying prospective tenant who touches everything:

- **Run the water** in every sink and the shower. Check pressure and how long hot water takes to arrive. Flush the toilets.
- **Test outlets** with a phone charger. Older buildings sometimes have dead ones nobody's mentioned.
- **Open and close** every window, door, and cabinet. Windows that don't seal are a heating bill problem.
- **Check your cell signal** in a few rooms. Thick walls and basement units can be dead zones.
- **Look for water damage** — stains on ceilings, warped baseboards, a musty smell in closets or under sinks. These suggest ongoing problems, not past ones.
- **Ask about pests** directly, and look for the evidence: droppings in cabinet corners, roach traps tucked behind appliances, gaps under doors.
- **Find the laundry, trash, and mail** situation. Is laundry in-unit, in the building, or three blocks away?

Then step outside the unit and think about the daily reality. How far to transit or parking? Is there street cleaning that'll tow you weekly? What's the neighborhood like after dark? Is the building's front door secure, or does it stick open?

## Read the Lease — All of It

This is the part people skip. Don't. Ask for a copy in advance and read it somewhere you're not being watched. A landlord who won't send you the lease to review beforehand is telling you something.

**The money terms.** Confirm the exact rent, the due date, and the grace period (if any). Find the late fee and how it's calculated — a flat $50 is different from 10% compounding. Check whether rent increases are specified for any renewal period, and note what payment methods are accepted. Some leases quietly forbid the method you'd prefer or add fees for online payment.

**The term and what happens at the end.** When does the lease start and end? Is there an automatic renewal clause? If so, how much notice do you need to give to *avoid* renewing, and when does that window open? Month-to-month conversion is common, but so is auto-renewal for another full year, and missing that deadline is expensive.

**Early termination.** Life happens — new jobs, breakups, family emergencies. Find out what breaking the lease costs. Is it a fixed fee (often one or two months' rent), or are you liable for rent until they re-rent the unit? Are there protected exceptions in your area, such as for military deployment or domestic violence situations? Many places have these by law regardless of what the lease says.

**The security deposit.** Note the amount, and check what your local law says about maximums — some jurisdictions cap deposits at one or two months' rent. Then find the return terms: how many days after move-out, and under what conditions? A good lease specifies that you'll receive an itemized list of any deductions. Also look for language distinguishing "normal wear and tear" from damage. Faded paint and minor carpet wear after two years is wear. A hole in the drywall is damage.

**What's actually included.** Utilities are the big one. Which are covered, which aren't, and how are shared utilities split? If the building has one water meter divided among six units, you're subsidizing whoever takes forty-minute showers. Also confirm in writing: parking, storage, appliances (is that washer staying?), internet, and any amenities like a gym or pool that might carry separate fees.

**Maintenance and repairs.** Who's responsible for what? Look for anything shifting normal landlord responsibilities onto you — clauses making tenants responsible for appliance repairs, plumbing, or HVAC servicing. Find out how to submit a request, whether there's an emergency number, and if any response timelines are promised. Ask what happens if something major breaks and the unit becomes uninhabitable.

**Entry rights.** Your landlord generally needs to give notice before entering except in emergencies — often 24 or 48 hours, depending on where you live. Check what the lease says and whether it matches local law.

**Rules and restrictions.** Pets (including fees, deposits, breed or weight limits, and whether "no pets" covers service animals — it usually can't). Smoking. Guests and how long they can stay. Alterations, including whether you can paint or mount a TV. Quiet hours. Subletting and whether short-term rentals are prohibited. If the building has an HOA or separate rules document, ask to see it — it's often incorporated into the lease by reference, meaning you're bound by rules you haven't read.

**Renters insurance.** Many leases require it now. Check the minimum coverage amount so you can budget accordingly. Even if it's not required, it's cheap and worth having.

## Watch for Clauses That Shouldn't Be There

Some lease provisions are unenforceable, but landlords include them anyway hoping tenants won't know better. Depending on your jurisdiction, be skeptical of:

- Waivers of your right to sue or to a habitable dwelling
- "Landlord not responsible for any damage" blanket clauses
- Automatic forfeiture of the entire deposit regardless of condition
- Non-refundable "cleaning fees" stacked on top of a deposit
- Permission to enter at any time without notice
- Requirements to pay the landlord's legal fees regardless of who wins a dispute

Finding one of these doesn't necessarily mean the landlord is malicious — plenty of small landlords use outdated template leases they downloaded years ago. But it's worth asking about, and it tells you something about how carefully this operation is run.

## Vet the Landlord, Not Just the Apartment

You're entering a year-long relationship with this person or company. Do some homework.

Search the property management company's name plus "reviews" or "complaints." Look at what current tenants say, especially about repair response times and deposit returns. If you see the same complaint repeatedly, believe it.

If you can, talk to someone who lives in the building. Catch a neighbor in the hallway. "Hey, I'm thinking about renting here — how's the management?" People are usually honest.

Confirm you're dealing with the actual owner or their authorized agent. Property records are often publicly searchable, and rental scams — where someone lists a property they don't control and collects deposits — are real. **Never send money for a unit you haven't seen in person**, and be very cautious about any request for wire transfers, gift cards, or cash-only payments.

## Get Everything in Writing

This is the single most important rule. If the landlord says they'll repaint before you move in, replace the broken blinds, or let you keep a cat despite the "no pets" clause — that promise is worth nothing unless it's in the lease.

Write it into the document itself or attach a signed addendum. "We'll take care of it" is not a contract term.

## Document the Move-In Condition

Before you unpack a single box, do a walkthrough with your phone. Photograph and video everything: every wall, floor, appliance, countertop, window, and especially any existing damage. Timestamp it. Get close-ups of scratches, stains, dings, and chipped paint.

If there's a move-in inspection checklist, fill it out thoroughly and get a copy signed by the landlord. If there isn't one, make your own, email it to the landlord, and keep the sent message. That email timestamp is your evidence.

This is your best protection against deposit disputes eighteen months from now, when nobody remembers whether that carpet stain was already there.

## A Few Last Things

**Keep copies of everything.** The signed lease, all addenda, receipts for the deposit and first month's rent, and every maintenance request you submit. Communicate in writing when you can — email creates a record that phone calls don't.

**If you have roommates, understand your liability.** Most leases make tenants "jointly and severally liable," meaning if your roommate stops paying, the landlord can pursue you for the full rent. A separate roommate agreement between you doesn't change your obligation to the landlord, but it helps sort things out between yourselves.

**Know that your local law matters more than the lease.** Tenant protections vary enormously by country, state, and even city — deposit limits, notice periods, rent control, eviction procedures, repair obligations. A quick search for your city's tenant rights, or a call to a local tenant union or housing authority, is time well spent. Many areas have free tenant hotlines. If you're signing something expensive or unusual, a short consultation with a local attorney costs far less than a bad lease.

**And if the pressure feels artificial, it probably is.** A landlord who won't give you a day to review the lease is a landlord who won't give you a day when the heat goes out in January. Good units come around. Walk away from the ones that require you to sign blind.

*This post is general information, not legal advice. Rental laws vary significantly by location — check the rules where you live.*
